why this mattered

It's an ordinary evening, and Mia can't find the search icon.

Her iPad is propped on a small table to her left tonight, her right wrist wrapped tightly in a cast. She opens Wikipedia the way she does most evenings, and scans the page, noticing a subtle difference in the look and feel of it. The toolbar at the bottom looks different now. The icons are floating, over whatever's scrolling underneath them, brightening and dimming as the page moves. Her first thought is that it looks sleek, almost beautiful. She taps where the search icon used to be, and for a second she can't tell if she got it, because she couldn't quite tell where the icon actually was, a phantom limb kind of certainty about exactly where something should be, with none of the feedback to confirm it's actually there.

She's not the only one who runs into Apple's Liquid Glass revolution that week. Across town, Arun is doing something similar for a different reason. He needs a source for a paper due in the morning, and he's typing a search term with a hand that doesn't always land exactly where he means it to. He finds the entry, taps in, and starts scanning for the section he needs. A pop-up surfaces over the text, translucent, hovering there uninvited, gone before he can decide whether to read it. He isn't sure if he closed it or if it closed itself. He starts scanning again, from the top.

Accessibility.

Neither of them did anything wrong. Both of them ran into Liquid Glass, the translucent, light bending material Apple rebuilt iOS around this year. Toolbars, tab bars, sheets, menus. Every system surface now floats over whatever sits behind it, brightening and dimming as the page scrolls, reflecting light like something physical. Apple built it to feel alive under a finger. Nobody built it with Mia's blurred center vision in mind, or with Arun's unsteady hands in mind. A system wide material doesn't get built for one reader. It ships to everyone, the same way, all at once.

Wikipedia runs on that same interface now, which means every reader inherits the changes Apple's Liquid Glass makes to contrast, to structure, to how confidently they can tap the thing they meant to tap. For most people, that's nothing worth noticing. For someone like Mia or Arun, it can be the difference between successfully finishing an article and begrudgingly giving up on it.

Wikimedia Foundation and Pratt Institute wanted an answer before Liquid Glass reached every reader like Mia and Arun, so we set out to test it across the two things people do most in the app. Reading an article, and searching for one. Liquid Glass was designed to be looked at. We needed to know if it could be universally usable.

how we tested it

A checklist finds the failures. Not the person who hits them.

We ran two methods side by side, not because one wasn't enough on its own, but because each one covers the other's blind spot. A WCAG audit is systematic. It moves through every screen in the search and reading flows and checks it against the standard, regardless of who is using it. It catches what fails on paper, not what it feels like to reach for a control and find it isn't responding the way it should.

A cognitive walkthrough is different. It means stepping through the same two flows the way Mia or Arun actually would, tap by tap, and noticing where a screen that technically passes still gets in someone's way. Some of what we found only shows up this way. A target that's technically 24 by 24 pixels but sits right where a tremor lands, a confirmation that's timed for someone who isn't fighting a cast.

persona-based

Our scope stayed narrow on purpose. Reading an article, and searching for one, the two things people do most in the app. In practice that ran as a specific sequence, not two methods happening in parallel. Personas came first, built from GOV.UK's accessibility persona methodology and Wikimedia's own mobile persona research, so the constraints we were testing against existed before a single screen got reviewed. Then came the audit itself. Every screen across both flows mapped against WCAG 2.1 and 2.2, with the pages to audit identified and screenshotted early so the whole team was working from the same set. Audit and walkthrough work then split across the five of us, to prevent one person's blind spots from becoming the whole team's blind spots.

Every failure that surfaced afterward went through the same two-part filter. Does this block or exclude someone with a disability outright, and how easy would it actually be to fix. A finding that scored high on both became Tier I. Something that mattered but was harder to justify fixing immediately, or wasn't as severe, dropped to Tier II or III.

who we tested against

Every finding got checked against two people, not an average.

Mia and Arun aren't composites built to make a point. They're grounded in the accessibility persona methodology GOV.UK uses for service design, and shaped further by Wikimedia's own mobile persona research into who actually reads on this app. Between them they cover four access needs at once: low vision, situational one-handed use, autism, and a motor tremor, which meant every screen we tested had to hold up for more than one kind of person at a time. Designing for just one of them would have meant designing around a phantom limb, certain a control is there because it always has been, blind to the reader for whom it never quite is.

what we found

Thirteen failures, grouped by what actually breaks, not by checklist order.

Powered by what Arun and Mia's cognitive walkthroughs and the WCAG audit turned up together, every finding below carries a tier tag and the WCAG success criterion it fails, Tier I blocks access outright, Tier II is a significant barrier, Tier III is worth fixing but survivable, and the columns are ordered by exactly that scale. The tier follows the same two factor rubric from earlier. How badly something excludes someone, weighed against how quickly it could realistically be fixed. A few of these thirteen turned up more than once across Mia's and Arun's separate walkthroughs, independent confirmation that made the case for including them stronger. Reading left to right moves from the most urgent failures toward the ones worth fixing but survivable, so the order carries meaning before a single card gets read.

Tier I · critical
05findings
Blocks access outright.
F01WCAG 1.4.1
Links only look like links because they're blue
Why it mattersMia's blurred central vision flattens subtle color cues fastest of all. A link that only reads as blue can disappear into body text before she ever gets to it.
FixConsistent bolder weight on every inline link, so it reads as a link even without color.
F02WCAG 1.4.11
Icons thin out exactly where the glass gets busiest
Why it mattersLow vision already asks Mia to work harder for contrast. Liquid Glass adds a second variable on top of that, contrast that shifts with whatever's scrolling underneath.
FixBolder icon strokes engineered to hold contrast across variable blur, not just the default background.
F03WCAG 4.1.3
VoiceOver announces the tap. It doesn't announce what happened
Why it mattersAnyone navigating by VoiceOver alone has no other way to find out. A search that quietly returned nothing looks identical to one still loading.
FixLive-region announcements for each of the five silent states, so the outcome is heard, not just the tap.
F04WCAG 2.2.1
Save confirmations don't say the article is actually available offline
Why it mattersMia is reading one-handed with her arm in a cast. A snackbar that vanishes on its own timer gives her one shot to catch information she needs, with no way to ask for it again.
FixA persistent, dismissible confirmation that states offline availability outright.
F05WCAG 2.5.1
Photo galleries give no indication there's more than one image
Why it mattersArun's walkthrough flagged this exact moment: nothing on screen tells him a gallery is even swipeable. Without a visible cue, the feature might as well not exist for him.
FixSingle-pointer navigation, arrows, dots, or a counter, instead of swipe-only browsing.
Tier II · significant
05findings
A significant barrier.
F06WCAG 2.5.8
Some icons ask for more precision than a tap can promise
Why it mattersArun's essential tremor turns a few extra pixels into the difference between hitting the target and mistapping into something else, every single time.
Fix24 by 24 pixel minimum tap area via padding, without necessarily resizing the icon itself.
F07usability
Type a search, and the answer hides under your own thumbs
Why it mattersMia and Arun hit this independently, on the same step, in separate walkthroughs. The system responds correctly and neither of them can see that it did, the featured walkthrough below goes deep on why.
FixReposition highlighted matches above the keyboard, or auto-scroll so the match stays visible.
F08WCAG 1.4.13
Pop-ups have no explicit way to close them
Why it mattersArun relies on controls behaving predictably to stay oriented. "Tap somewhere else and hope" is the opposite of a clear, explicit action he can trust.
FixA visible close button on every overlay, not just a tap-outside gesture.
F09usability
Help and Support is buried, not absent
Why it mattersOf the seven questions asked at every step of both walkthroughs, "will help exist if she gets stuck" came back no for Mia and Arun alike, every single time.
FixRelocate it into primary navigation instead of a nested settings path.
F10WCAG 2.4.1
No way to skip straight to a relevant article section
Why it mattersArun rereads and reorients constantly by design, that's how he builds a mental map of a topic. Repeating the same navigation block every time he does adds friction he shouldn't have to pay.
FixMove header navigation earlier in tab order and add a skip-to-content link.
Tier III · survivable
03findings
Worth fixing but survivable.
F11WCAG 2.5.1
Search history options only reveal themselves on a swipe
Why it mattersA drag gesture asks for a level of control Arun's tremor doesn't reliably give. A single tap on a visible button doesn't.
FixReplace the swipe with tap-to-reveal, or an overflow menu.
F12WCAG 1.4.10
Content overflows the viewport at larger text sizes
Why it mattersLarger text is exactly the accommodation Mia relies on for her low vision. It shouldn't be the setting that breaks the layout on her.
FixEnsure proper reflow once text size increases, rather than horizontal scroll.
F13usability
Search context resets every time you return from an article
Why it mattersMia's walkthrough put a number on this: two extra taps and a full cognitive reorientation, every mistap, compounding over a 20 to 30 minute reading session.
FixPreserve query, results, and scroll position across the back navigation.

The shape of the fix strategy follows the tiers already laid out. Most of what failed under 1.4 Distinguishable, bolder links, thicker icon strokes, is a styling pass, which is why it clusters into Tier I alongside criteria that are technically urgent but cheap to fix. The four fails under 2.5 Input Modalities are more of a mixed bag. Some are the same kind of padding fix as the touch target findings, but dragging with no single-pointer alternative and a map compass with no way to turn off motion sensing are closer to real engineering work, which is part of why 2.5 failures land across every tier instead of just one.

There were two fails outside those two groups that finished the final picture. No mechanism to skip repeated navigation blocks, and a login flow that shuts out password managers and Face ID entirely. Neither is large in scope, but both are real barriers on their own, which is exactly the kind of criterion a pass/fail count alone would bury next to the bigger clusters.

a walkthrough in action

One finding, followed all the way down to the tap that broke it.

The audit results above tell you what failed. They don't tell you what it felt like. Let's step into Mia's shoes and walk through this one ourselves, tap by tap, the way she actually would. This is the walkthrough step behind finding F07. Task 2, Step 4 of Mia's cognitive walkthrough, searching within an article. Not a moderated session with a real participant, this is an expert walkthrough built from her persona, stepping through the same task she would, tap by tap, against the same seven questions asked at every step of the audit.

“
This is a significant failure. The whole point of in-article search is to see the matched word, and hiding it under the keyboard defeats the feature.
walkthrough note · Task 2, Step 4
1Does Mia understand what to do?
Yes. The find function executes exactly as expected.
2Can she find it?
No. The navigation arrows are there, but they don't bring the matched word into view.
3Does she understand what happened?
No. The match itself is hidden behind the keyboard, invisible unless she manually scrolls.
4Can she avoid a mistake?
Partially. She can't confirm she's on the right match, because she can't see it.
5Does anything break her focus?
Yes, and that's the problem. The keyboard is what's covering what she's searching for.
6Does she need to remember anything, or get help?
No burden to remember from a previous step, but no help surfaces either if she gets stuck.
2×
This wasn't a one-off. Arun hit the same wall independently, on the same step of his own reading flow: the matched word stayed hidden behind the keyboard for him too. Two personas, two separate walkthroughs, the same failure both times.

Every step in a walkthrough gets asked the same seven questions, no matter which task or which persona is stepping through it: does she understand what a control is and how to use it, can she find it, does she understand what happened once she uses it, can she recover from a mistake, does anything pull her focus away, does she need to remember something from an earlier step, and is help there if she gets stuck. Task 2, Step 4 answers four of those seven with a flat no or a qualified partial, which is what pushes a single step from ordinary friction into a finding worth its own tier.

The method itself borrows from how people actually learn an unfamiliar interface. Not by reading instructions, but by trying an action and checking whether it moved them closer to their goal, the exploratory model of learning that Clayton Lewis, Cathleen Wharton, John Rieman, and Peter Polson helped shape into the cognitive walkthrough method as we know it today. Donald Norman gave the two places that trial and error breaks down their names. The gulf of execution, the gap between intending to do something and figuring out how, and the gulf of evaluation, the gap between what the system just did and whether a person can tell. Find and Understand content are both evaluation failures here. Mia performs the right action, the system responds, and she still can't see that it did.

That gap costs her more than it would a reader with no visual impairment. Cognitive load theory treats working memory as a limited resource, and every extra second she spends scanning for a hidden match, or scrolling to confirm she landed on the right one, is capacity already spent compensating for her blurred center vision. It's the same reach for a limb that isn't there, certain the control exists because it always has, spending effort just to confirm what shouldn't need confirming. A feature that technically works and one that works but stays invisible look identical on a checklist. They don't feel identical to the person holding the phone.

where it landed

Delivered to Wikimedia. Landed exactly as planned.

Four weeks closed with a shareout to Wikimedia Foundation stakeholders on May 12. The full set of thirteen tiered findings, the 27-criterion WCAG 2.1/2.2 compliance table behind them, both personas and their cognitive walkthroughs, and a prioritized recommendations framework built to hand straight to an engineering team. Delivered as a client engagement through Pratt Institute, not a personal project shipped on our own schedule.

Stakeholders walked away with everything an engineering team would need to start acting on this without another round of discovery. Every finding tiered and tied to a WCAG citation, both personas' walkthroughs documented step by step, and a fix strategy already sequenced by impact and effort. That's exactly where a strong audit is supposed to land at handoff, validated by the process that produced it, and specific enough to build from on day one.

what I'd carry forward

The audit caught what's broken on paper. The walkthrough caught what's broken in use.

Running both methods side by side is what made this document work. The audit caught the failures a walkthrough would never stumble into on its own, no single-pointer alternative buried in a saved-articles list, a login flow that quietly shuts out Face ID. The walkthrough caught the failures a checklist would have quietly passed, like the in-article search highlight that never fails a named WCAG criterion but still fails the moment you actually try to use it. Neither method alone gets you the whole picture.

What I'd want to add next isn't a fix for something broken, it's a next step this project earned. Moderated sessions with real people living with low vision, a tremor, or autism, testing this on their own devices, with their own assistive technology and their own workarounds. Mia and Arun got us further than a checklist alone ever could. Real participants are what would take this from a strong audit to something tested against the people it's actually for, in hopes that Mia finally finds the limb she was missing the first time she reached for search.

1.3Adaptable 2 criteria
1.3.4OrientationAAPass

All pages operable in both portrait and landscape.

1.3.5Identify Input PurposeAAPass

Only one input field for search, and it only appears on the search page; its meaning is understood.

1.4Distinguishable 7 criteria
1.4.1Use of ColorAFail

Blue links within pages are not differentiated by anything but color. Not underlined, same weight as regular text. Colorblind and low vision users may not distinguish them. Some links carry an external-link icon and differ by weight, but not all of them.

1.4.3Contrast (Minimum)AAPass
1.4.4Resize TextAAPartial Pass

Passes through the phone's own accessibility settings, but fails Wikipedia's own resize-test function. Menus and pop-ups don't resize, only the article text does.

1.4.10ReflowAAFail

Content can be scrolled horizontally once text size increases; some pages have horizontal scrollability when they shouldn't.

1.4.11Non-Text ContrastAAFail

Select icons are thin and sit inside the glass menu; contrast is affected as the glass menu morphs whatever content it's overlaid on.

1.4.12Text SpacingAAPass

Good spacing between lines and characters.

1.4.13Content on Hover or FocusAAFail

On the article page, the "more options" pop-up disappears only when tapping something else, with no clear way to close it. In focus mode on the map page, the pop-up has no close button and can only be dismissed by clicking elsewhere; buttons inside that pop-up can't be tapped either. The text-size tool pop-up has the same issue.

2.1Keyboard Accessible 1 criterion
2.1.1KeyboardAPartial Pass

All interactive elements are reachable and operable via VoiceOver navigation except one component on the places-search map page. Places search, select place, and the user can't select individual buttons inside the resulting pop-up.

2.2Enough Time 2 criteria
2.2.1Timing AdjustableAFail

Time-limited snackbars carry actionable controls, like "Add to list," that disappear automatically with no way to extend, pause, or recover them.

2.2.2Pause, Stop, HideAPass

No continuously moving or auto-updating content in the reading or search flows.

2.4Navigable 6 criteria
2.4.1Bypass BlocksAFail

No mechanism exists to skip repeated page blocks.

2.4.3Focus OrderAPass

Tab order proceeds meaningfully.

2.4.4Link Purpose In ContextAPass

Text links, search results, and icons all have clear purpose from context.

2.4.6Headings and LabelsAAPass

Headings and subheadings are clear, descriptive, and accurately reflect the content and purpose of each section.

2.4.7Focus VisibleAAPass

A visible focus indicator, a black square moving sequentially through each section, clearly shows focus location while tabbing.

2.4.11Focus Not Obscured MinimumAAPass

Focused elements stay at least partially visible while navigating with VoiceOver; the interface scrolls appropriately to maintain visibility.

2.5Input Modalities 5 criteria
2.5.1Pointer GesturesAFail

A three-finger swipe is required to scroll the page, with no single-pointer equivalent; scrolling relies exclusively on a multi-point gesture.

2.5.2Pointer CancellationAPass

All interactive elements trigger on touch-up, so a tap can still be cancelled by moving the finger away before release.

2.5.4Motion ActuationAFail

No way to switch off motion on the compass element in map search; it can only be used by physically turning the phone.

2.5.7Dragging MovementsAAFail

Multiple places require dragging with no single-pointer equivalent.

FailImages in articles, drag or swipe is required to move between images; no arrow buttons or nav dots offer a single-tap alternative.
FailSaved articles section, drag or swipe is required to reveal options like delete; no long-press menu or options button offers a tap-based alternative.
PassDouble tap to zoom, a single-pointer action with no dragging involved.
ReviewMaps, dragging is required to pan and explore, which may be essential to how a map works; needs confirmation on author-controlled versus user-agent-controlled behavior before a final verdict.
2.5.8Target Size MinimumAAFail

Three interactive targets in the article toolbar fall below the 24 by 24 CSS pixel minimum.

FailBookmark button, 16 × 23px, both dimensions below minimum.
FailSearch button, 19 × 25px, width below minimum.
FailMore options button, 23 × 18px, both dimensions below minimum.
FailMap landmark dots, 17 × 17px, below minimum on both dimensions, though this may fall under the essential exception since size is tied to geographic position.
3.2Predictable 1 criterion
3.2.6Consistent HelpAPass

A help and feedback mechanism exists inside app settings, and it's located consistently across every screen.

3.3Input Assistance 2 criteria
3.3.7Redundant EntryANeeds Review

Returning to search after navigating to an article doesn't persist the search term, requiring it to be retyped. This may not be a traditional redundant-entry failure, since it could be read as a new search session, but it could still be a barrier for some users. Popular search engines behave the same way, so the team flagged it for further discussion rather than a final verdict.

3.3.8Accessible Authentication MinimumAAFail

The login process doesn't support password manager autofill or biometric authentication; users must type credentials manually with no alternative, a barrier for users with cognitive or motor disabilities. Account creation passes. A CAPTCHA is present, but a "Request Account" option exists for anyone unable to complete it. Most core functionality, search and saving articles, is accessible without an account at all, which softens the impact.

4.1Compatible 1 criterion
4.1.3Status MessagesAAPartial Fail

Several status messages aren't communicated to VoiceOver without the user manually moving focus to them.

PassSave / bookmark, correctly announced by VoiceOver without requiring a focus move.
FailSearch results, "no results found" isn't announced by VoiceOver; discovering the outcome requires manual navigation.
FailReading list, adding an article isn't announced by VoiceOver despite a visual confirmation appearing on screen, inconsistent with the save action, which is announced correctly.
