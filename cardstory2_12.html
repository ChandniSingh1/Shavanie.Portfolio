// Discovery + Build · iOS · Client Project · Feature Sprint

CardStory is a pre-launch app for Pokémon TCG collectors, built to help them track, buy, and sell cards in one place. It had all the right pieces of a solid deck: tools to track a collection, ways to hunt for rare finds, real value on the line, but it was missing one card that made the whole thing playable, a marketplace. Two months before launch, the founder brought us in with a critical problem: users struggled to navigate the app, couldn't find where to buy cards, and had no reason to trust the sellers on the other end.

I led the end-to-end design of CardStory's marketplace feature, the most complex gap our research surfaced. Starting from zero, I designed a trust-first buying experience that included structured browsing, seller transparency, pricing clarity, and engagement signals. My CS background shaped every decision: I understood what was technically feasible and designed within real constraints instead of against them.

the user experience

You're a Pokémon card collector.

You track market prices, hunt rare finds, and manage a collection worth real money. You download CardStory, an app built specifically for you. But the moment you try to buy a card, everything breaks down. Listings are scattered, pricing is inconsistent, and there's no way to know if the seller is trustworthy.

You don't complete the purchase. You leave.

This is what we found, again and again. CardStory had real potential as an all-in-one collector platform, but without a centralized marketplace and clear trust signals, users couldn't do the one thing that mattered most: buy and sell with confidence. It comes down to a basic principle of trust in commerce, which is loss aversion. Loss aversion is when people weigh the risk of losing money to a stranger far more heavily than the reward of a good find. Without visible signals of trust, hesitation wins by default.

The data pointed to one underlying issue.
Collectors had an app.
They didn't have a marketplace.

CardStory let users browse and track cards, but the moment purchase intent kicked in, the experience had no answer for it. Everything lived in the same undifferentiated space, cards for sale sitting next to cards you were just tracking, sitting next to portfolio tools. It was like keeping your holo rares and your bulk commons in the same binder page: technically organized, but no way to tell what's actually for sale. No hierarchy. No separation. No signal telling users what was buyable and what wasn't.

Users weren't failing because the app was broken. They were failing because a piece of the experience simply didn't exist.

The problem was clear.
The why wasn't.

What wasn't clear was why, and more importantly, which part of the broken experience was costing CardStory the most. Before redesigning a single screen, we needed to understand the full shape of the failure.

We recruited collectors ranging from casual fans to serious traders and sat them down with moderated usability tasks, no leading questions and no hints, just people trying to buy a card two months before launch. One by one, the app came apart like a house of cards.

what we found
01 / finding
NO DEDICATED MARKETPLACE MEANT NO CLEAR PATH TO BUY
CardStory was built to let users browse, buy, and sell cards, all in one unified place. That worked in theory, but when cards for sale, cards you were just tracking, and portfolio tools were all mixed together with no real space between them, it led to confusion. Users weren't failing to purchase because the app was broken. The path to buy was buried under a pile of cards, with no way to dig it out.

02 / finding
PRICING CONFUSION DESTROYED PURCHASE CONFIDENCE
Users ran into two different kinds of prices throughout the app: listing prices and market values. The problem was that there was no clear visual distinction between them. Cards showing $0.00 made users question whether the data was accurate at all, which led every number to blur together into one big, unreliable feed.

03 / finding
UNKNOWN SELLERS MEANT ABANDONED PURCHASES
There wasn't a marketplace to begin with, so there was no way to see seller ratings, verification, or transaction history, nothing to signal that a stranger on the other end of a listing could be trusted with real money. That's the same loss-aversion problem from earlier showing up again, this time at the crucial moment of payment, and for a platform built around high-value collectibles, this was a hard dealbreaker.

design decisions
decision 01

A Dedicated Marketplace Without Restricting Commerce Elsewhere

Testing surfaced two distinct user behaviors: organization mode and acquisition mode. Think fire type versus water type: two different elements, two different mental states that clearly don't mix. One space can't serve both without creating friction for each.

decision_01.diff 2 changes
✕ REJECTED

Combine both spaces into one unified feed. Use filters and labels to distinguish purchasable from non-purchasable cards.

filters can't solve a mental model problem. Ambiguity persists regardless of labeling.

✓ SHIPPED

A dedicated Marketplace section, where each and every card inside is completely buyable.

Jakob's Law. Users trust a new pattern faster when it mirrors one they already know, and a dedicated marketplace tab reads instantly: eBay and TCGPlayer already taught people what it means.

A dedicated Marketplace tab creates one clear threshold: every card inside is buyable, every price actionable, and every seller is relevant.

decision 02

Separate and Label Every Price with Context

5 of 5 users misread prices, not because they couldn't see them, but because they couldn't interpret them. It's like comparing two Pokémon by attack stat alone: one shows 3000, the other shows 40. The bigger number looks like the obvious winner, until you realize you don't know the type matchup or which fight you're even looking at. Visibility wasn't the problem. Meaning was.

decision_02.diff 2 changes
✕ REJECTED

Make the price larger and more prominent so users can see it more clearly.

visibility wasn't the problem, meaning was. A bigger number that users still can't interpret doesn't build confidence.

✓ SHIPPED

Two values per card: current market price and 30-day movement. Color-coded. Sourced. Unambiguous.

Cognitive load theory. Every extra second spent decoding a number is a second of doubt, and two clearly labeled values remove the guesswork so trust builds before the user even decides to buy.

Every card now shows two values, current market price and 30-day price movement with color-coded trend indicators. Validated against eBay, TCGPlayer, and competitor platforms.

decision 03

Build Trust Into the Marketplace Infrastructure

Users weren't just asking, "Is this seller good?" They were asking, "Should I trust this platform at all?" A star rating answers part of that, but not all of it. So what does? A trust ecosystem does.

decision_03.diff 2 changes
✕ REJECTED

Add a simple star rating to seller profiles.

a single number doesn't answer the question users were actually asking. A full ecosystem can.

✓ SHIPPED

The trifecta of trust: Seller Spotlights, Verified Badges, full Profile Pages. Remove one and the whole system weakens.

Social proof. People trust a decision more when several independent signals agree, not when one number tries to carry the weight alone.

Three layers working together, Seller Spotlights for discovery, Verified Badges for credibility, Seller Profile Pages for proof. Remove any one and the system weakens.

decision 04

Multi-Photo Listings with Full-Screen Zoom

For collectors, condition is everything: a one-point grading difference can mean hundreds of dollars. A single photo with no zoom turned every purchase into a mystery pack pull, pay first, find out what you actually got after. It asked users to make a financial decision blindfolded.

decision_04.diff 2 changes
✕ REJECTED

Add more images to each listing.

more images without zoom still leaves users guessing on condition. Both were needed together.

✓ SHIPPED

Multiple angles plus full-screen zoom, simulating in-person card inspection, digitally.

uncertainty is the biggest tax on conversion. When users can verify quality themselves, hesitation drops, the decision no longer depends on trusting a stranger's description.

Every listing now supports multiple angles plus full-screen zoom, simulating the in-person card inspection experience digitally.

V1 tried to fit three cards per row, more browsing surface, more discovery, in theory. The moment it was tested, the problems were immediate: cramped touch targets and card details competing for space that didn't exist. One participant called it "a wall of cards."

The issue wasn't aesthetic, it was cognitive load, the same problem from the pricing decision, just showing up in the layout this time. Too much information in too little space forced users to work harder to extract meaning from every card.

We brought the two-card layout back to the same group of collectors and ran the same browsing and purchase tasks. This time, the complaints from round one didn't come back.

Less to process per glance was enough to change how people felt about browsing the marketplace at all.

the fix

Dropping from three cards per row to two gave each card room to breathe, touch targets became tappable, prices became readable at a glance.

the validation

Dropping density didn't just fix a layout complaint, it confirmed the bigger bet: the marketplace needed room to breathe more than it needed volume.

My CS background reinforced this call. On mobile, it was always going to be a layout problem, not a content problem.

This became the final layout, no further changes were needed after this round.

where it landed

From a critical gap to a shippable marketplace.

Two rounds of testing carried the marketplace from a shoebox of loose, unsorted cards to a binder where every page is labeled and every card has its place. A dedicated Marketplace tab replaced ambiguity with one clear threshold. Two-value pricing replaced guesswork with meaning. A three-layer trust system replaced a single number with real signal. Multi-photo zoom replaced blind trust with verification. None of it was designed in isolation. Each decision closed a specific gap the research had already named.

These weren't concepts pitched cold. Each one had already been tested, rejected in an earlier form, and validated against real behavior before it reached the founder. That's most of why the marketplace direction was approved to move straight into development. Nothing here was a guess dressed up as a design.

The marketplace is validated, not yet live. What exists today is two rounds of testing against real collectors: the original findings that surfaced the problem, and a second round that confirmed the fix held. Right now, it's a strong pull under controlled conditions. The real test is the open market: does the same clarity that worked in a moderated session hold up once real money and real strangers are involved? That's what happens once CardStory ships to the App Store.

the final design

Every decision, shipped together.

The marketplace tab, pricing clarity, trust system, and multi-photo listings, all four decisions working together as one experience.

what I'd carry forward

The hardest part wasn't any single screen.

It was resisting the instinct to fix four different trust and clarity problems with one quick move. Every wrong instinct in this project was the fast, obvious one: filters instead of a separate space; bigger numbers instead of clearer meaning; a star rating instead of a trust system; more photos instead of a way to actually inspect the card. The slower answer took longer to justify and held up every time it was tested.

My CS background showed up less in the visuals than in the structure underneath them: what data the app already had to work with, what could realistically ship in an 8-week sprint, what the information architecture needed to hold before any screen got drawn. Designing inside those constraints, instead of around them, is why every decision here can be defended in one sentence instead of explained away in five.

If I took this further, I'd want production numbers, not testing-room ones: completion rate on the new flow, time from search to first purchase, and, most importantly, whether the trust system actually lowers hesitation the way five collectors in a moderated session suggested it would. In short: whether it holds under real money and real strangers is the one card I haven't played yet.
