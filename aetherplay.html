// Pratt Institute · INFO 680 · Gesture-to-Sound-to-Sight AR Instrument

AetherPlay is a browser-based AR instrument. Point a webcam at your bare hands and it reads the motion, then turns it into a sound and a shape at the same time. Nothing to wear, nothing to install. It went through three complete builds over six weeks. It started as a bouncing cat. It grew into a hybrid of animals and rough sacred geometry. It settled, by the end, into a full two-player geometry piece, with each build tested extensively on eight people.

We designed and fine-tuned the gesture vocabulary across all three builds, and I co-ran the testing that decided what survived each round. The recurring finding never really changed: the hand never needed a new trick. Every real win lived in what that motion turned into.

01 / insight
→
Instant Feedback Wins

The cat's real strength was never its nine gestures. It was reacting the moment a hand moved. That's a feedback loop, cause and effect with no delay, and it's what let people play without a single instruction.

02 / insight
✦
Thin-Slicing Effect

A friendly cartoon read as a kids' toy, no matter how real the interaction underneath actually was. This is called thin-slicing, when people decide whether something is serious within a few seconds of looking at it, long before they've touched anything.

03 / insight
◆
Shared Play Landed

A six-character lobby code turned a solo demo into something two people built together. That one addition changed how every other feature got used.

how it started

It started with wanting to turn someone else's music down.

Picture this: it's Sunday, 10pm, and you're in crunch mode with your co-designers, racing to get a deliverable ready for a stakeholder presentation at 8am tomorrow. A faint, persistent buzz starts coming through your speakers. It's not loud, but it's constant, and it works its way into your thoughts until you can't quite hold onto one. You shuffle through your open tabs, sure it's a leftover YouTube video you forgot to close. It isn't. It was Lilly, my classmate and co-designer, with music playing low to help her concentrate, leaking through her mic for the whole meeting. Half-joking, exhausted, I said I wished I could just reach through the screen and turn it down myself. The joke landed wrong, but thankfully in a good way, and the three of us spent the rest of the call, deadline briefly forgotten, sketching what "reaching through the screen" could even look like.

What we built first was small. A cat sprite bounced when you pinched, one gesture, one sound. It worked well enough that we brought it to a professor at Pratt for an honest read. His verdict was blunt: gesture plus sound is a fine pitch, but it isn't an AR pitch. If it could ship as a phone app with a camera taped on, the AR wasn't doing anything.

tech breakdown

Fusion starts with four tools that don't know each other yet.

Before we could make them agree on anything, we had to get honest about what we were actually trying to fuse. Each one has its own inputs, its own outputs, its own sense of time. Four tools, four different jobs. One reads a hand. One listens. One draws. And one puts it all in the room with you. Here's what each one actually does on its own, before we get into how they talk to each other.

ARaugmented reality
// augmented reality
AR.render(world + layer)
Digital content layered on top of the real world, in real time, through a camera. No headset here. Just a browser and a webcam standing in for a screen you can reach through.
MediaPipehand tracking
// hand tracking
MediaPipe.detect(hand)
Google's on-device machine-learning model. Reads 21 landmark points per hand from the webcam feed, live, on-device. No server, nothing leaves the browser.
Tone.jsaudio engine
// audio engine
Tone.play(gesture.note)
A Web Audio framework built for the browser. Turns a detected gesture into a synthesized note, live, with no plugins, no external hardware, no instrument in the room.
p5.jsvisual engine
// visual engine
draw(gesture.shape)
A creative-coding library for canvas rendering. Takes the same gesture data Tone.js reads and draws it instead: particles, shapes, motion, sixty times a second.

Now that we know what each tool does individually, we can get into the real work: making these three tools agree on one timeline. MediaPipe reads your hand off the webcam and classifies it into one of nine gestures, gesture.dict. The instant it names one, Tone.js and p5.js both fire on that same frame. One plays a note, and the other morphs that into a shape. One detection, two outputs, synced by construction instead of by luck. All of it runs in the browser, with no install, no headset, and no app store needed. Nothing here is a model reacting after the fact. The same detection event triggers both outputs directly, so there's no chance of the sound and the shape drifting out of step with each other over time. That single decision, sync by construction, is the reason the whole thing feels like one instrument instead of two apps that happen to run near each other.

The whole system lived in a single CodePen, a free online code editor that runs HTML, CSS, and JavaScript live in the browser with no local setup required. Roughly half the gesture math I wrote quickly by hand, and the other half I vibe-coded even faster, then went back in to actually understand and tighten what I'd shipped. Keeping the whole build to three panes, one HTML, one CSS, one JS, with no build step at all, wasn't a technical limit so much as a discipline. Every dependency I didn't add was one less thing that could break mid-demo in front of a room. By the third build that CodePen had grown considerably, but it never once needed an actual build step to run.

the first build

Pinch, and the cat bounced. That was it.

v1_cat_prototype.mov modified x
// drop yours in here

If the gap between hand and sound were audible at all, nothing else mattered. We built it that fast on purpose, not because we were rushing but because speed was the whole test. The cat sprite alone proved it, one pinch and one sound, nothing else needed to sell the idea. Everything after this point was refinement, not reinvention.

From there it was addition. Nine named gestures in total, plus a theremin layer where hand height and distance shaped pitch continuously instead of snapping to fixed notes. The sound underneath was a marimba patch modeled after Animal Crossing's dialogue chimes. Warm and a little playful, an instrument you're allowed to be bad at. Then we put it in front of eight people who had no idea what it was. Picking those chimes as a reference wasn't random. That game trained a whole generation to associate warm, forgiving sound design with feeling welcomed instead of judged, which is exactly the register we wanted for a system prone to the occasional misread. We didn't tell any of the eight what to expect. That was on purpose, because the only reaction worth trusting was the one nobody had time to perform, the one that happened before they'd even thought to be polite about it.

"
I wasn't thinking about gestures. I was just poking the cat and stuff kept happening.

We tested two modes on eight people. One was open-palm-only, needing no vocabulary at all. The other was the full nine-gesture set. Mode one worked in about thirty seconds, no instructions needed. Mode two is where it got honest. Some gestures sat too close together on a phone screen to hold apart.

The cat's real win was a feedback loop. The instant it mirrored a hand, people reacted before they'd even thought about the sound. But several testers asked, independently, if this was made for kids. It wasn't. The interaction was serious. The cat just didn't look like it.

the pivot

Scratch that. Let's go back to the (AR) drawing board.

Both things were true, and only one could survive. We kept the part that made people react without thinking: a hand moving something instantly. We cut the part that made them ask if this was for kids. That's the aesthetic usability effect working against us. A friendly look made people doubt how serious the interaction underneath actually was. The gesture vocabulary barely changed, a pinch still triggered, a palm still held. What changed was commitment. It took two more passes to figure out exactly how far from the cat we actually needed to go.

the second build

More animals. More flips. More sound. Still not the whole answer.

The second build didn't jump straight to pure geometry. It kept animal characters, more of them this time, and wrapped each in a glowing sacred-geometry aura instead of a flat outline. That's a hedge, a bet that keeps both options alive instead of committing fully to either. The real addition was the flip. A gesture could now send an animal into a full flip, and each flip fired its own layered sound on top of whatever was already playing.

On paper, it was the safer bet to keep the characters people already liked, dress them up, and give the hand a bit more to do. Whether that was actually the right call was the whole point of testing it.

But dressing the cat up in geometry didn't answer the same question the last round raised: was this a toy, or an instrument. We were betting that flips and layered sound would tip it toward instrument, but that was a bet only strangers testing it could actually settle, not our own hunch.

So we put it back in front of eight people, half returning from round one and half brand new. We watched closely for where the hedge held up and where it didn't. What came back was as split as the bet itself.

It's cool, but I can't tell if I'm playing with an animal or a shape.

The flips landed immediately. People started chaining them just to hear what stacked on what. But the hybrid look didn't settle the toy-or-instrument question, it just relocated it. That's categorical ambiguity. When something doesn't sit cleanly in one mental bucket, people hesitate on what it even is.

Once the novelty of the flips wore off, testers ran through the sound palette just as fast as before. It held their attention a little longer than the cat had, but it was still exhausted within ten minutes. Keeping the animals hadn't resolved anything. It had just softened the question, and the only way to answer it for real was to drop the characters entirely.

the final build

No more hedging. The animals left completely, and everything else finally arrived.

v3_geometry_final.mov modified x
// full sacred geometry, duet mode and the sound rework all landed this round
Full Sacred Geometry
Proper lighting, particle wakes, a slow ambient rotation so the shape never looks frozen between plays. The character work is gone. The geometry has to hold attention entirely on its own now.
Aesthetic–Usability Effect People rate things that look more beautiful as more usable and more trustworthy, before they've touched a single control. A shape that visibly catches light and moves on its own reads as a finished product, not a prototype, the instant it appears.
v3_geometry_detail.pngmodified
Duet Mode
Two people share a session through a six-character lobby code, each hand tracked locally into the same shape. The moment testers could hand the code to whoever was next to them, the session stopped being a demo.
Shared Attention Experiencing something at the same time as another person makes the experience itself feel more intense. The emotion doesn't just add up, it amplifies. Handing someone a lobby code turns a solo gesture demo into a moment two people made together.
v3_duet_lobby.pngmodified
Keepsake Trace
At the end of a session, AetherPlay renders a still image of your gesture trail as a download. A performance that used to just disappear the moment you stopped playing.
The IKEA Effect People value things more when they had a hand in making them. A downloadable trace converts a few minutes of gesture into an artifact, something worth keeping because you made it, which is exactly the kind of thing people screenshot and send to a friend.
v3_trace_download.pngmodified
Layered Sound
One small patch became a full layered palette: warm pads under a percussive top line, deep enough that non-musicians said they could finally make a song with it.
Flow & Self-Efficacy Flow depends on a tight, rewarding loop between action and feedback. Layering the sound gives every gesture a bigger, more musical payoff, which is why non-musicians said, for the first time, that it felt like playing a real instrument instead of triggering a sound effect.

Nobody asked if this was for kids this time. The geometry finally did the job it was built for. Duet mode was the standout. The moment testers could hand the code to whoever was next to them, the session stopped being a demo and became something two people did together.

The sound was the real surprise. Testers who'd never played an instrument were the ones most excited by the new palette, and several stayed well past their ten minutes. That's flow state, when an activity absorbs enough attention that time itself stops being tracked.

the wrap

The hand was never the hard part. Everything it touched was.

Three builds and three rounds of strangers later, the fusion the brief actually asked for held. Gesture, sound, and shape finally worked as one complete experience, not three technologies running near each other. One detection firing two synced outputs. A duet mode that turned solo play into something shared. A sound palette rich enough that non-musicians said they could finally make a song. The hand's job never changed across any version. What it triggered did, and that's where every real win and every real miss actually lived.

The bigger lesson wasn't technical. A friendly cartoon undid more trust than any bug ever could. Testers doubted the interaction before they'd touched anything, purely on how it looked. I went in assuming the hard problem was synchronization. It turned out to be just as much about what a shape silently promises before anyone plays with it.

Three builds later, the shapes finally listen back.

carrying this forward

What I'd carry forward: the shared-attention trick behind duet mode wasn't specific to AR at all. Any tool that can invite a second person into a solo moment probably should.

What's next, if I keep going: a gesture vocabulary that doesn't need eight strangers and a facilitator to learn it. Hand tracking that holds up in worse lighting than a demo room ever has, too.
